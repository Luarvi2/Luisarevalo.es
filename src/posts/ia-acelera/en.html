This week I read a piece of news that made me stop for a moment. Anthropic, the company behind the AI Claude, has updated the prospectus for its stock market listing. Among the financial figures, which is what almost everyone looked at, there were two facts that seem far more important to me.

The first: in September, more than 90% of the code that went into the company was written by its own AI. And each engineer contributed, on average, more than 45 times as much code per day as in 2024.

The second: the company itself admits it could be about to reach what its internal rules call “drastically accelerated progress attributable to AI”. In other words, using AI to build a better AI, which in turn builds an even better one, faster and faster.

“We do not expect to be able to safely use Claude to fully autonomously design and build successive generations of frontier models without meaningful human oversight or understanding.”

The risk that struck me most

It isn't that AI becomes very clever. It's something simpler and more of an engineer's concern: that progress moves faster than the ability to measure it. The data the company uses to track that pace arrives with a delay, so it might not notice a sharp acceleration until it has already happened.

Imagine driving at night with a speedometer that shows how fast you were going ten minutes ago. On a quiet straight road, nothing happens. The trouble starts exactly when you speed up.

What I learned in aviation

When a new aircraft is tested, nobody goes out on day one and flies it to the limit. You “expand the envelope” step by step: a bit faster, a bit higher, a slightly tighter turn. And at every step, before taking the next one, you look at the data. With instruments that tell you what is happening now, not what happened yesterday.

The underlying rule is simple: you never fly faster than you can measure. If the instruments lag behind, you don't accelerate. You stop and fix the instruments.

That's why I think it's a good sign that an AI company says this in an official document, in front of investors, rather than in a nice press release. What remains to be seen is whether it acts on it when doing so costs speed against the competition.

And outside the labs?

Most of us will never build AI models. But the same problem is already in our companies, on a different scale.

  • More code isn't better code. Each engineer producing 45 times more is impressive, but as I wrote in Leading AI isn't counting PRs, volume says nothing about value. If we produce 45 times more, we need to review better, not 45 times faster.
  • Review becomes the bottleneck. When the machine writes almost everything, the human job becomes understanding, checking and deciding. It's less visible, but that's where responsibility lies.
  • You have to measure in real time. If a team suddenly starts delivering much faster, do its quality indicators notice this week, or in three months?

Where I think this is heading

I think over the next year we'll see more companies boasting about how much of their code their AI writes, and few explaining how they review it. Regulators and investors will start asking for the latter. And a highly sought-after profile will emerge: people who don't write the code, but know how to check whether what the machine did is correct, safe and what was actually needed.

In aviation, we never leave the autopilot alone in the cockpit. Not because it isn't good, but because someone has to be watching the instruments. It should be the same with AI, especially when it starts to accelerate.

A question for you: in your work, which part are you already handing over to AI, and how do you check that it turns out right?
