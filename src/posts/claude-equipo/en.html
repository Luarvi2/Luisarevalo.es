A few days ago I was at a conference about Claude, Anthropic's AI. Several people who use it every day shared their tricks, and one of them showed me something I haven't been able to stop thinking about: at their company, Claude isn't a tool each person opens on their own laptop. It's one more member of the team, with its own seat in the chat where everyone works.

A referee inside the channel

They use Slack, and in Slack you can mention Claude just like you'd mention a colleague. They have a channel with the whole team and Claude in it, with a very clear role:

  • it takes meeting notes and records what was decided and who owns each task;
  • it keeps track of progress, so anyone can ask it how something is going;
  • and it nudges whoever hasn't delivered what was due, so nobody has to be the nag.

He described it as a referee: it doesn't play the match, but it knows what minute it is, who has the ball and when someone has broken a rule. And because it sees everything, it's the one best placed to say where the team stands.

Coding without opening a laptop

The second part blew me away. To change something on their platform or build a new tool, this person doesn't sit down at a computer. He talks to Claude, from his phone for example, and Claude connects on its own to the GitHub repository where the code lives, prepares the changes and publishes them.

I say this with a smile, because this very website works the same way. I'm not a programmer. I explain to Claude what I want, as I'm doing with this article, it prepares the changes on GitHub and I review them before they go live. My computer can be switched off.

Watching systems the way you watch an aircraft

The third idea was real-time visibility. They have bots that constantly check how their systems are doing: errors, database outages, data going missing. Everything lands in the same Slack, so the team finds out in the same place where they're already talking.

This reminded me a lot of aviation. A modern aircraft sends data from its engines and systems to the ground while it's flying. If something starts to go wrong, the maintenance team knows before the plane lands and can have the part ready. The point isn't to react when something breaks, but to find out before it does.

Why this hits close to home

In my projects, a huge share of the time went into the stuff nobody sees: minutes, to-do lists, "how's your part going?" messages. At Eurofighter I coordinated IT, four partner companies and NATO, and keeping everyone up to date was almost a job in itself. If someone had kept that ledger for me, I could have spent that time deciding and solving problems.

A few weeks ago I wrote about an AI envoy for every team. What I saw at the conference is that same idea, already running in a real company.

What I'd keep an eye on

  • Someone reviews before it goes live. AI preparing changes without you opening a laptop is fantastic. AI publishing them without anyone looking is something else. In aviation no change flies without a second signature, and I'd do the same here, at least for anything important.
  • It reminds without overwhelming. One timely reminder helps. Ten a day get ignored, and the same goes for system alarms: if everything beeps, nothing does.
  • The referee doesn't become the boss. Claude can keep score and remind people, but priorities and decisions have to come from humans.
  • It only gets access to what it needs. A new colleague doesn't get every key on day one. Same with AI.

Where I think this is heading

I think it will soon be normal to have AI inside the team chat, not as a separate window everyone uses their own way. What will change most isn't how fast we code, but the coordination: knowing where everything stands without having to ask. And people's work will shift towards deciding, reviewing and owning what gets published.

A question for you: if your team had a colleague tomorrow who takes notes, chases deliverables and watches the systems, what's the first thing you'd hand over?
